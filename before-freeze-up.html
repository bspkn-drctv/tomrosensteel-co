How Tom Rosensteel Decides What Surface Work Cannot Wait for Spring

Why the end of the surface season is sorted by what winter will do to a failure, not by how bad it looks, and how the deferred list becomes the snow crew's operating instructions.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

Every fall, commercial property managers in the Twin Cities are handed a list of surface problems that is longer than the time left to fix them. Cracked walks, a settled dock apron, a catch basin structure that has started to sink, a drive aisle that held water all summer. The season for concrete and asphalt work is closing, and not everything on the list is going to make it.

Tom Rosensteel, General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), spends the last weeks of the surface season making exactly that call. TCOS has served commercial properties across the Twin Cities Metro since 1997, and Rosensteel's view is that the decision about what gets done before freeze-up is more consequential than most of the work itself. The wrong item done in the wrong order is money spent twice: once now, and once in spring when the thing that should have been done first has finished failing.

The Question Is Not What Is Broken. It Is What Winter Will Do to It.

Rosensteel does not sort the fall list by how bad something looks. He sorts it by what a Twin Cities winter will do to it between now and April.

Some failures are stable. A slab with hairline shrinkage cracking that has been that way for several seasons is going to look the same in spring. A depression that drains, even if it is unsightly, is not getting worse in the cold. Those can wait, and pretending otherwise burns the short remaining season on work that had a full year of life left in it.

Other failures are on a clock. Anything that lets water in and holds it there is going to be a different problem after a winter of freeze and thaw cycles. An open joint next to a building, a crack that has started to separate, a sunken catch basin structure with a gap around it, a curb that has broken loose and lets meltwater run under the pavement behind it: each of those is a small repair in the fall and a section replacement in the spring. The cost difference is not marginal, and the schedule difference is worse, because the spring version of the job is competing with every other spring job in the market.

Problem-solution, that is how we approach every client relationship. Identify the issue, present the fix, execute, and follow up. Property managers want a partner they can rely on, not one who disappears after the contract is signed.Tom Rosensteel

Three Buckets, Decided Before the Weather Decides Them

The way Rosensteel runs the end of the surface season, every open item on a property lands in one of three places, and each has a different definition of done.

  • Close it before frost. This is the water work and the safety work. Sealing open cracks and joints so water has no path into the base. Replacing a catch basin structure that has failed, along with the concrete around it, so the drainage point is not the point where the pavement collapses. Correcting the trip hazards on walks and entrances that a winter of ice and reduced visibility will turn into claims. None of this is glamorous. All of it is what decides the size of next year's list.
  • Defer it to spring, on purpose. Full section replacements, large asphalt areas, and anything that would have to cure at the edge of the temperature window. Concrete placed in conditions nobody would choose does not become good concrete because the calendar was inconvenient. Rosensteel would rather write a scope now, hold it, and pour it properly in May than rush a pour in late October and repair it in June. The key word is on purpose: deferred work is scheduled, priced, and on the owner's budget, not forgotten.
  • Leave it and watch it. The stable failures. Documented, photographed, and re-read at the next walk, with a note on what would move them into one of the other two buckets.

What separates this from an ordinary punch list is that the deferred and watched items come with a reason attached. A property manager who is told why a section is waiting for spring can defend that decision to ownership. A property manager who is simply told it did not get done cannot.

Winning is a habit, unfortunately so is losing. You control the process or the process controls you. That applies to every route, every project, every outcome.Tom Rosensteel

Telling the Snow Crew What the Concrete Crew Left Behind

There is one more step that Rosensteel considers non-negotiable and that most surface programs skip entirely: the deferred list goes to the winter operation.

Every item that was left for spring is a spot on the property that the snow and ice crews are about to run over, stack on, or salt for five months. A heaved slab that will be replaced in May is also a plow blade hazard in December. A settled area that holds water is also the refreeze point that generates ice complaints all winter. A section of pavement with a weak base is exactly where a heavy delivery vehicle should not be turning, and exactly where a pile of snow should not be sitting.

When the same operator is responsible for both seasons, the fall surface decisions become winter operating instructions. Plow routes are adjusted to lift the blade over a marked heave. Stacking locations move off a section that is scheduled for replacement. Material use is watched on newer concrete that has not been sealed. The alternative, two providers who never speak, is how a deferred repair becomes a larger one and how a winter of damage lands on the wrong invoice.

I surround myself with people who are the best at what they do, in snow, in surface work, in landscaping. My job is not to be the expert in every trade. My job is to build the team, set the standard, and hold everyone accountable to it.Tom Rosensteel

Why He Makes the Call Early

The temptation in a short season is to keep working until the weather stops the work. Rosensteel treats that as a planning failure rather than a show of effort. A crew still placing concrete when the first hard frost arrives is a crew that was scheduled by hope.

His practice is to set the decision date before the temperature does. By a fixed point in the fall, every property has its three buckets settled, the close-before-frost work is sequenced, and the spring scope is written and in front of ownership while the capital conversation is still happening. That timing matters more than most property managers realize. Spring surface capacity in this market is booked by people who decided in the fall. The ones who wait until the damage is visible in April are choosing from whatever is left.

Property managers are accountable to their tenants, their ownership groups, and all stakeholders in the process. They need a Partner who documents everything, responds to calls immediately, and owns the outcome. That level of accountability is often missing in this industry. It shouldn't be.Tom Rosensteel

What a Property Manager Should Have in Hand Before the Ground Freezes

  • A list sorted by what winter will do, not by what looks worst. If the sort order is cosmetic, the water problems are being deferred by accident.
  • A named reason next to every deferred item. Curing conditions, budget cycle, and base condition are reasons. Ran out of time is a symptom.
  • The spring scope, written now. A deferred repair without a scope is a repair that will be discovered again, not scheduled.
  • Confirmation that the snow plan knows about the deferred spots. Heaves, weak sections, and low areas should appear on the winter site plan as things to avoid, not as surprises.
  • Photographs and a date for the watched items. A stable failure is only stable if someone is checking.
  • A decision date, not a weather date. The season ends when the plan says it ends. Letting the first frost make the decision means it was never made.
