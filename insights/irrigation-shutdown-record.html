Why an Irrigation Shutdown Is Only as Good as the Record It Leaves

Why freeze damage starts below grade before the surface looks frozen, why the backflow assembly is the part to watch, and why the written zone record from the fall shutdown is what makes spring startup routine.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

Irrigation winterization gets far less attention than anything else on a commercial property in the fall. Parking lots are being readied for plows and landscape crews are working through leaf pickup, so the irrigation system tends to sit at the bottom of the list. It is also one of the fastest ways for a small job to turn into a much larger repair once the ground freezes.

Tom Rosensteel is General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), which serves commercial properties across the Twin Cities metro area only. TCOS handles grounds maintenance, including irrigation shutdown, as part of a full-service approach alongside snow and ice management. Irrigation belongs on the same fall list as the rest of a property's winter preparation, and the reasons why are worth understanding before the first hard freeze.

The Damage Starts Below Grade

Water left in a pipe, a valve or a backflow assembly does no harm all summer. Once it freezes, it expands, and components that worked fine for months crack. In Minnesota the freeze line reaches most shallow irrigation lines well before the ground surface looks frozen. That is the trap. The cue most property managers wait for, a frost warning in the forecast, arrives after the risk has already started.

On a commercial site with several zones, a single missed shutoff is rarely a single broken head. It can mean a cracked mainline, a split backflow preventer, or ruined valve solenoids sitting just below grade. In the spring, that turns a routine startup into replacing an entire zone's piping.

What a Complete Shutdown Covers

A proper commercial winterization is a sequence, and each step closes a different gap.

  • Water off at the main. The supply is closed at the source, not just at the controller, so no residual pressure feeds the system while it sits idle.
  • Every zone blown out on its own. Compressed air pushes the remaining water out through the heads one zone at a time. A skipped zone, or air pressure that was rushed, is the most common reason a cracked lateral line turns up at spring startup.
  • Backflow drained and protected. The backflow assembly is often the most exposed and least insulated part of a commercial system, and the most expensive single piece to replace. It has to be fully drained, and an above-ground assembly needs insulation or a heat-traced wrap through the winter.
  • Valve boxes and controller checked. Valve boxes are inspected for standing water before they freeze shut, and the controller is set to off so nothing tries to run a cycle on the first false-spring day.
  • The zones written down. A zone map and condition notes taken at shutdown and left in the property manager's file.

The first four steps protect the system through the winter. The last one is what makes spring work.

The Record Is the Spring Instructions

The written zone record is the step most likely to be skipped, because nothing breaks without it. Its value shows up months later. A technician who knows how many zones exist, which ones had problems at shutdown and where the backflow assembly sits can finish a startup inspection in a fraction of the time it takes to rediscover the system from scratch. On a property that has changed ownership or management companies since the last season, that record is what keeps spring startup from being guesswork for whoever inspects it next.

The record also exposes an incomplete shutdown. A property manager calling an irrigation contractor in the middle of winter for a check usually has a shutdown that was not finished the first time: a backflow assembly that was drained but never insulated, or a zone skipped because a valve did not open on the first pass and nobody went back to confirm it. A system that was shut down correctly should not need attention again until a controlled spring startup. The quiet months are quiet because the fall work was done right, not by accident.

Why a Portfolio Misses Sites

A facility director with one site can track one system. A property manager running retail centers or office parks across the metro has a harder problem: many systems, each with its own zone count and age, and often a different original installer. Without a consistent shutdown record across every site, one or two properties get missed every year, usually the ones with the least turf frontage or the smallest visible heads, which are easy to pass on a quick fall drive-through.

The fix is the same one that works for snow contracts and concrete inspections: one vendor, one shutdown window, one written record per site. When the crew already on site for fall cleanup or pre-season snow preparation can complete the shutdown on the same visit, the coordination gap between a separate irrigation-only contractor and everyone else working the property goes away. Having the same vendor handle spring startup keeps the shutdown and startup notes with one team instead of losing them between two companies.

Waiting for the Forecast Is Waiting Too Long

The practical pressure is scheduling. A shutdown needs a qualified crew, and a frost warning is too late to start booking one. Properties that wait for the weather to force the decision are competing for the same limited blowout appointments as every other property that waited.

What a Property Manager Should Take From It

  • The freeze reaches shallow irrigation lines before the surface looks frozen, so the shutdown has to be scheduled ahead of the cue most people wait for.
  • A complete shutdown is a sequence: water off at the main, every zone blown out on its own, backflow drained and protected, valve boxes and controller checked, zones written down.
  • The written zone record is what turns spring startup into a routine visit instead of a rediscovery.
  • On a portfolio, one vendor, one shutdown window and one record per site is what keeps the smaller sites from being missed.
  • When comparing bids, confirm every step is included, not just the blowout.

An irrigation system that went into the winter properly is usually forgotten until spring. That is the point. The question for a property manager is whether the fall shutdown left enough on paper for spring to be routine.
