Why the Thermometer, Not the Calendar, Closes the Asphalt Season

Why asphalt needs warm nights to compact and bond, why a project pushed into November usually waits until spring, why paving is lined up ahead of snow and ice management, and what separates a lot that is assessed from one that is scheduled.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

Most deadlines on a commercial property are set by people. A lease renews on a date, a budget closes on a date, a vendor is told to be finished by a date. The deadline for asphalt work is different. It is set by the overnight temperature, it moves with the weather, and no amount of scheduling pressure pushes it back. Twin Cities commercial properties still waiting on asphalt patches have a narrow window left before overnight temperatures rule the work out for the season, and the thing that closes the window is not a calendar page but a thermometer.

Tom Rosensteel is General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), which serves commercial properties across the Twin Cities metro area only. TCOS crews are scheduling the last paving and resurfacing jobs of the year now for parking lots and drive lanes across the metro, lining up the work before snow and ice management takes over. The reasoning behind that sequence is worth spelling out, because it explains why an asphalt decision deferred by a couple of weeks in the fall can turn into a decision deferred by half a year.

Asphalt Needs Heat to Become a Surface

A patch or a resurfaced lane is not finished when the material is placed. It is finished when the material has been compacted into a dense, bonded layer and has cooled in that state. Both of those steps depend on the asphalt staying warm long enough to be worked. Once nightly lows settle near freezing, asphalt will not compact or bond the way a lasting surface needs. The material cools faster than the crew can densify it, the new patch does not knit to the old pavement around it, and what looks finished in the afternoon is already compromised by the next morning.

That is why the deadline is a temperature and not a date. A warm stretch in late October can still carry a project. A cold snap in the same week can close it. The property manager who is waiting for a convenient week on the calendar is waiting on the wrong variable.

What Pushed Into November Usually Means

The source of most asphalt disappointment is the assumption that a late project is simply a slightly later project. In practice, a project pushed into November usually waits until spring instead. There is no half measure between the two. Either the material goes down while the nights are still warm enough for it to cure into a lasting surface, or it does not go down until the nights are warm enough again, and in the Twin Cities that is months away.

For a property manager that gap matters in ways that go beyond the pavement itself. A patch that was supposed to close out this year's maintenance budget moves into next year's. A drive lane that was going to be resurfaced before the plows arrived spends the winter as it is, which means every pass of a plow blade and every freeze and thaw cycle works on the damage that was already there. The work that eventually gets done in spring is, at best, the same scope at a later date. Often it is a larger scope, because the winter has had its say.

Why Paving Comes Before the Plows

TCOS lines up the last paving and resurfacing jobs of the year before snow and ice management takes over, and the order is not incidental. Snow and ice work and surface work compete for the same lots, the same drive lanes and the same weeks on the calendar. Once the season turns, the lot has to be available for plowing and treatment, and a paving project that is still open at that point is in the way of the winter operation rather than ahead of it.

Finishing the surface work first also means the snow crews inherit a lot that is ready for them. A freshly placed patch that has cured properly is a surface a plow can run over. An unrepaired pothole or a failing section of drive lane is a hazard the plow has to work around, and one that the winter will keep enlarging.

The Lot That Still Has a Chance

None of this means the season is over. A commercial lot assessed and scheduled this week still has a real shot at going down before the ground gives out for winter. The word that matters in that sentence is scheduled. An assessment that ends with a report sitting in an inbox has not moved the property any closer to a finished surface. An assessment that ends with a date on the crew calendar has.

That is the practical shape of the decision in front of a property manager with open asphalt work this fall. The question is not whether the work is worth doing. It is whether the work gets done while the nights still allow it, or whether the property accepts that it will carry the defect through a winter and revisit it in spring with whatever the winter has added.

What a Property Manager Should Take From It

  • The asphalt deadline is set by overnight temperatures, not by the calendar. Once nightly lows settle near freezing, asphalt will not compact or bond the way a lasting surface needs.
  • A project pushed into November usually waits until spring. There is no slightly later version of fall paving.
  • Surface work belongs ahead of snow and ice management, because the two compete for the same lots and the same weeks, and a cured surface is what the plows should inherit.
  • A lot assessed and scheduled this week still has a real shot at going down before winter. Assessed alone does not.

The weather will decide when the window closes. What the property manager decides is whether the work was on the schedule when it did.
