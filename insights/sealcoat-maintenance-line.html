Why Sealcoating Belongs on the Maintenance Line, Not the Discretionary One

Why the cost of a skipped sealcoat cycle arrives years later, how crack sealing, sealcoating, patching and overlay work as stages rather than competing services, why next fall's window is booked months ahead, and what a bid has to specify to protect the lot.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

The real cost of skipping a sealcoat cycle on a Twin Cities parking lot does not show up the year it is skipped. It shows up two or three years later, which is exactly why it gets deferred. Sealcoating is one of the least expensive line items in a parking lot's life cycle, and it is also the one most often skipped. Because the bill arrives later rather than immediately, the line is the first one cut when a budget gets tight.

Tom Rosensteel is General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), which serves commercial properties across the Twin Cities metro area. TCOS handles commercial snow and ice management, landscape maintenance, concrete repair and asphalt work under one roof, which is why a sealcoating bid can be folded into the same conversation as the winter snow contract instead of being managed as a separate vendor relationship.

What the Coating Is Actually Doing

Sealcoating is a protective liquid coating applied over existing asphalt to slow down the two things that destroy a parking lot: UV exposure and water infiltration. Minnesota asphalt takes a harder hit than asphalt almost anywhere else in the country because it cycles through freeze and thaw dozens of times a season. Water gets into hairline cracks, freezes, expands, and turns a cosmetic crack into a structural pothole in a relatively short period. A properly timed sealcoat slows oxidation, blocks water penetration, and buys several more years before the asphalt needs a more expensive fix.

Unsealed asphalt in the Twin Cities oxidizes and becomes brittle roughly twice as fast as sealed asphalt exposed to the same freeze and thaw cycles. A lot that should have gone another eight years on a normal sealcoat cycle can need a full mill and overlay in five, and a mill and overlay runs many times the cost of two sealcoat applications over the same period. That is the arithmetic behind treating sealcoating as a scheduled maintenance line rather than a discretionary expense that gets cut when the budget is tight.

Stages, Not Competing Services

Crack sealing, sealcoating, patching and a mill and overlay are not competing services. They are stages. Crack sealing addresses individual cracks before they let water in, and it is done as needed between sealcoats whenever the crack is dry. Sealcoating is surface-wide protection against UV and water on a two to three year cycle. Patching is for localized failure, potholes and alligatored sections. A mill and overlay is for widespread structural failure across the lot, and it replaces the full top layer at the highest cost of the four.

A lot on a healthy maintenance plan gets crack sealing as needed, sealcoating on its cycle, and patching only for isolated damage. Skipping sealcoating does not necessarily save the crack sealing or patching budget. It potentially accelerates both.

Most Twin Cities commercial lots do well on a two to three year sealcoating cycle. High-traffic lots with heavier vehicle loads, such as loading docks and grocery anchor lots, often need it closer to every two years, while a lot that sees mostly passenger vehicle traffic in an office park can sometimes stretch to three. The right interval depends on traffic volume, drainage, and how well crack sealing has been kept current between coats.

Why the Calendar Has to Be Set Now

The window that works best for an existing lot is late August through early October, after the asphalt has had a full summer to dry out and before overnight temperatures fall below the cutoff for proper cure time. Sealcoating applied too late in the fall does not cure fully before the first freeze and can peel the following spring. New asphalt needs several months to cure before it can be sealcoated, so a lot paved in the spring waits until the following season.

That window is narrow, and contractors with sound fall crew capacity book up quickly once the weather turns. A property manager who waits until mid-September to call around is competing with every other commercial lot trying to beat the same cutoff. Booking in July or early August, even for a September or October service date, secures a spot on the schedule before the rush and leaves room if a weather delay pushes the date. For a property manager working from an annual capital plan, that makes sealcoating a scheduled line decided well ahead of the season, not a call made in September.

Property managers who wait until the lot looks bad are usually a year past the point where sealcoating alone would have solved the problem, and they are looking at patching or an overlay instead.

What the Line Item Should Buy

A bid built to protect the lot, rather than to win the job on the lowest number, should specify square footage measured on site rather than estimated from a satellite image, a coal tar or asphalt emulsion sealer specified by name and application rate, crack sealing called out as a separate line item completed before the sealcoat goes down, and a cure time before the lot reopens to traffic. A bid that skips crack sealing before sealcoating is sealing over a problem instead of fixing it, and that surface will fail early. Potholes and alligatored sections need to be patched first for the same reason: sealcoating over damaged asphalt traps water under the coating and accelerates the failure it was meant to prevent.

Sealcoating covers existing striping completely, so restriping belongs in the same project scope rather than a separate call weeks later. Accessible parking markings, van-accessible spaces and directional arrows all need to be reapplied to the same locations and dimensions as before, which means the existing layout should be photographed or measured ahead of the sealcoat so the restriping crew works from an accurate reference. Some sealcoat-only bids leave striping as an add-on, so it should be confirmed as included rather than assumed.

Most commercial sealcoat work in the Twin Cities is scheduled overnight or in phased sections so retail, office and multi-tenant properties do not lose a full day of parking access, but not every contractor offers phased closures. How phasing would work for a specific lot layout is a question for the bidding contractor before signing.

The Older Lot and the Paper Trail

Two questions property managers ask before scheduling sealcoating deserve direct answers. The first is whether an older lot already on a replacement timeline is worth sealing at all. Usually it is. Sealcoating an older lot on a documented replacement timeline still slows deterioration enough to avoid an emergency patch-and-repair budget in the interim, and it keeps the lot presentable and safer until the planned replacement.

The second is liability. Sealcoating alone does not reduce liability exposure. What does support a property manager's position when a slip-and-fall or vehicle damage claim comes in is a documented maintenance history across sealcoating, crack sealing, and snow and ice management: a paper trail showing the lot was actively maintained rather than neglected. A sealcoat on a schedule is one more dated entry in that record.

Sealcoating is deferred because the bill for deferring it arrives years later and under a different name. Putting it on the maintenance line, on a fixed cycle, booked before the fall rush, is how a property manager avoids paying for a mill and overlay years before the lot should have needed one.
