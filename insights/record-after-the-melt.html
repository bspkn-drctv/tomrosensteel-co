Why a Snow Program Is Judged Twice, and the Second Test Comes After the Melt

Why a commercial snow and ice program is judged on whether the lot was clear and, months later, on whether the property can prove it, what a usable service record has to carry, and why the documentation standard belongs in the contract rather than in a vendor's habit.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

A commercial snow and ice program passes or fails twice. The first test happens during the storm, and everyone on the property can see the result: the lot is clear enough for tenants, employees and customers to move safely, or it is not. The second test happens later, sometimes long after the snow is gone, when a slip-and-fall claim is filed and a carrier or an attorney asks the property one question: what proof do you have that the lot and the walkways were serviced?

Tom Rosensteel is General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), which serves commercial properties across the Twin Cities metro area only. TCOS builds GPS-documented, timestamped service records into every commercial snow and ice management contract it holds, covering lots, drive lanes, entrances and walkways as separate, trackable service lines, and the reasoning behind that is worth laying out for any property manager who has only ever thought about the first test.

Two Standards, One Program

The operational standard is the one a property manager lives with all winter. It is whether the lot was clear and whether people could get where they were going. That standard keeps the property running.

The evidentiary standard is different. It asks whether the property and its vendor can show what was done, and when. It only matters on the day a claim arrives, and that day can come months after the snow has melted. The property owner and the management company are the ones it protects.

The two standards are not the same thing. A lot can be cleared well on every storm of the season and still leave no usable record behind. On the day of a claim, a verbal assurance that the crew was there is not a record. A documented service history is.

Where the Proof Usually Lives

For a Twin Cities property manager, the answer to the proof question usually lives with whoever plows the snow. That is the practical problem. The record a property needs on the day of a claim is a record someone else kept, or did not keep.

Documentation practices vary widely from one vendor to the next, from a full GPS-verified visit log down to a simple invoice with no visit detail at all. A property manager carrying portfolio risk should confirm what a vendor actually produces before assuming it matches an industry standard, because there is no single standard to assume.

Insurance carriers have noticed. They increasingly ask for the service record directly during underwriting and at renewal, and a property with a documented winter service history is an easier risk to underwrite than one that can only say a vendor handles it.

What a Record Has to Carry

A date on its own tells a claims adjuster almost nothing. A date and a time, tied to the moment the contract's trigger depth was reached and the service that followed, tells them the vendor was responding to actual conditions on a schedule they can check. Where the contract sets a trigger and the record shows the crew responding at that threshold storm after storm, the consistency itself becomes part of the defense.

A timestamp means more when it is tied to a location. GPS confirmation establishes that the crew and the equipment were physically on site rather than logged as scheduled, and it matters most across a multi-site portfolio, where a property manager has no practical way to confirm service in person at every address during a storm.

Material matters too. When de-icing material goes down, the record should say what was applied, how much, and where, with parking areas, drive lanes, entrances, ramps and walkways noted separately. A property with icy walkways and heavily salted drive lanes tells a different story than a property with no application record anywhere.

That separation matters. Parking lots get plowed on a schedule. Entrances and walkways are where people are actually on foot, often carrying something, moving quickly, or stepping out of a vehicle into cold conditions. A record that documents the lot and not the walkway leaves the highest-risk surface on the property unaccounted for, which is why walkway and entrance treatment belongs on its own line and not as an assumption bundled into the lot service.

Where it exists, a timestamped photo of a cleared entrance or walkway tied to the same visit record adds a layer a log alone cannot. It is not always practical on every visit during an active storm, but when it is there, it is among the strongest evidence a property can produce.

Why the Clause Matters More Than the Habit

A vendor's internal documentation habit is not a property's protection, because an internal process can change from one season to the next. The standard works best written into the service agreement itself: a clause describing what gets recorded, how it is stored, and how quickly it can be retrieved. That clause is what turns "our vendor documents visits" into something a carrier or an attorney can actually rely on.

It is also the right moment to ask the hard questions. Can the vendor produce a full visit history for the property for the current season, not a summary? Is each visit tied to a GPS-confirmed location, or only to a schedule entry? Are walkways and entrances documented separately from the lot? How quickly can the record be produced if a claim is filed long after the melt? If the answer to any of these is a pause rather than a direct yes, that pause is a finding, not a formality.

What the Record Is, and What It Is Not

A GPS-confirmed, timestamped service record is a stronger starting point than a schedule or an invoice alone, because it ties a specific crew and vehicle to a specific location at a specific time. It is one part of a defense, not a guarantee. But its absence is often more notable than its presence, and records are worth keeping well past the season they were made, because a claim can surface long after the snow is gone.

  • A snow program is judged on two standards: whether the lot was clear during the storm, and whether the property can prove what was done and when, once a claim arrives.
  • The proof usually lives with the vendor, and documentation practices vary widely, so a property manager should confirm what a vendor actually produces.
  • A usable record carries a date and time, a GPS-confirmed location, the trigger and the service that followed, the material applied and where, with walkways and entrances on their own line.
  • The standard belongs in the contract as a clause, not in a vendor's internal habit, so it can be relied on when it matters.

The first test is visible from the office window. The second one is the reason the record exists.
