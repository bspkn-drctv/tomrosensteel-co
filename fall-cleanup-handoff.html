Why Fall Cleanup Is the First Job of the Snow Season

What leaf drop does to walkways, storm drains and mulch beds, why a cleanup schedule beats a single visit, and why the ground cleared in the fall is the same ground the snow crews and equipment will need within weeks.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

Leaf cleanup on a commercial property looks like the last task of the landscape season. On most Twin Cities properties it is better understood as the first task of the snow season, because the ground it clears is the same ground the winter crews will need within weeks. That is the reasoning behind the way Twin City Outdoor Services runs seasonal leaf and debris cleanup as part of its commercial landscape maintenance contracts across the Twin Cities metro, and it changes how a property manager should read the fall calendar.

Tom Rosensteel is General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), which serves commercial properties across the Twin Cities metro area only. Fall cleanup is one of the clearest examples of a seasonal job whose real purpose is the season after it.

What Leaf Drop Does to a Property

Fall leaf drop and debris on a commercial property can bury walkways, storm drains and mulch beds fast. A layer of leaves across a walkway is a nuisance on a dry day. A storm drain under that same layer is a different matter, because it is no longer doing its job. The leaves are not the hazard by themselves. The hazard is what they do to the things underneath them.

Then the fall storms roll through. Rain on a blocked drain has nowhere to go, so it stands. Wet leaves on a walkway or an entry apron stop being a nuisance and become a slip hazard for anyone crossing them. If the cleanup is not done on a schedule, the property spends the back half of fall with standing water and slick surfaces in exactly the places where people walk and where the drainage system is supposed to be working hardest.

Why a Schedule Matters More Than a Single Cleanup

The word that matters is schedule. Leaves do not drop all at once. They come down over weeks, and the storm that follows a cleanup brings down the next layer. A single cleanup timed to the calendar clears the property on that day and leaves it to accumulate again until spring. A scheduled program keeps walkways, drains and beds clear through the whole drop and through the storms between, which is the only way standing water and slip hazards stay off the property rather than reappearing after every front.

For a property manager, that distinction is the difference between a service line item and an outcome. The outcome is a property that stays safe and drains properly through the fall, not a property that happened to be clean on a particular morning.

The Ground the Snow Crews Need

Here is where the landscape season and the snow season meet on the same square footage. Parking lot islands, entryways and drainage areas are the spots fall cleanup clears. They are also the spots winter crews and snow equipment need once snow arrives. An entryway that still carries leaf buildup when the first snow lands gets plowed and shoveled with that debris under it. A drainage area that was never cleared goes into the freeze blocked, and meltwater behaves accordingly. Parking lot islands buried in debris are harder to work around and harder to pile against.

So the reason TCOS keeps those areas clear before the winter crews and snow equipment need them is not tidiness. It is that the snow operation inherits whatever condition the landscape season leaves behind. A property that finishes fall cleanup properly hands the snow crews clean ground to work. A property that does not hands them a problem that is now frozen in place.

Why the Scheduling Window Closes Early

The practical pressure is the calendar. Crews book solid for the fall season, and a property whose cleanup schedule is not locked in competes for the same limited time as every other property that waited. The leaves do not wait for an opening. Neither does the first storm. A cleanup schedule set early is a schedule the property actually gets. A schedule set late is a hope.

What a Property Manager Should Take From It

  • Fall leaf drop buries walkways, storm drains and mulch beds fast, and fall storms turn that buildup into standing water and slip hazards if it is not cleared on a schedule.
  • Cleanup is a scheduled program through the drop, not a single visit, because the next storm brings the next layer.
  • Parking lot islands, entryways and drainage areas are the same ground the winter crews and snow equipment will need. Clearing them in the fall is the start of winter readiness, not the end of the landscape season.
  • If a property's fall cleanup schedule is not locked in yet, now is the time, before crews book solid for the season.

The leaves come down on their own schedule. The question for a property manager is whether the cleanup is on a schedule too.
