What a Failing Parking Lot Tells You Before It Becomes a Capital Project

Freeze and thaw, drainage, and the difference between a repair that holds and one that buys a single season.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

Concrete and asphalt are the parts of a commercial property that everyone uses and almost nobody looks at until something goes wrong. A cracked apron at a loading dock, a sunken area that holds water after every rain, a curb line that has started to crumble: these register as cosmetic for a season or two, then they register as a capital request.

Tom Rosensteel, General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), works on the operational side of that problem. TCOS has served commercial properties across the Twin Cities Metro since 1997, and surface work is the division where the cost of waiting is easiest to measure and hardest to sell.

Water Is the Actual Problem

Concrete in Minnesota rarely fails because of traffic. It fails because of water and temperature acting on it together.

Water gets into a hairline crack, a joint that has lost its sealant, or the base under a slab. It freezes, expands, and widens the space it occupied. It thaws, seeps deeper, and does it again. Over a Twin Cities winter that cycle repeats many times, and each pass makes the next one easier. A crack that was cosmetic in October is structural by April, and the surface damage visible in spring is usually the last stage of a process that started underneath.

That is why drainage matters more than the surface material in most surface assessments. Standing water in a lot is not only a slip hazard and a nuisance for tenants, it is a delivery system for the damage. Low spots, blocked drainage paths, and deteriorated catch basin structures concentrate water exactly where the pavement is least able to handle it. Where a catch basin structure itself has failed, the fix is replacement of the structure and the concrete around it, not a cleaning.

Read the Failure Before You Choose the Fix

Different failures look similar from a car window and mean very different things.

  • Hairline surface cracking in concrete is common and often shrinkage from the original pour. It matters mostly because it is a path for water, so sealing it is preventive work rather than repair.
  • Cracks that have separated or shifted vertically mean something below the slab has moved. Filling them treats the symptom. The base, the drainage, or both are the actual condition.
  • Spalling and scaling, where the top layer flakes away, usually points at water sitting in the surface through freeze and thaw, often accelerated by deicing chemicals on concrete that was never sealed or was placed too late in the season to cure properly.
  • Alligator cracking in asphalt, the interconnected pattern that resembles hide, is a base failure showing through. An overlay on top of it buys a season and hides the diagnosis.
  • Rutting and depressions at dumpster pads, drive aisles, and dock approaches are load problems. They come back unless the section is rebuilt for the load it actually carries.

The practical point is that the same square footage can need sealing, partial replacement, or full removal and rebuild depending on what is under it, and the only way to know is to look before writing the scope.

Repair or Replace Is a Judgment Call, Not a Preference

There is no honest general rule for when a surface should be patched and when a section should come out. It depends on how much of the area is affected, whether the base is sound, how the section drains, what loads cross it, and how many seasons the owner intends to hold the property.

What a property manager should expect is that the recommendation comes with reasoning attached. A repair proposed without an explanation of what caused the failure is a repair that is likely to be proposed again in two years.

Winning is a habit, unfortunately so is losing. You control the process or the process controls you. That applies to every route, every project, every outcome.Tom Rosensteel

Winter Writes the Bill That Spring Pays

Surface work and snow work are usually bought as separate services and are physically the same asset.

Deicing material left on concrete, especially on newer pours and unsealed surfaces, accelerates scaling. Snow stacked over curbs, islands, and irrigation heads takes them out mechanically. Piles placed where meltwater runs back across a drive aisle create refreeze that shortens the life of that pavement and creates an ice hazard at the same time. Every one of those is a decision somebody made in January that turns into a surface line item in June.

That is a large part of the argument for having the same operator responsible for both. An operator who will be repairing the concrete in summer makes different choices about where snow gets stacked and how much material goes down in winter.

The Minnesota Calendar Is Short and It Does Not Move

Concrete and asphalt work in this market runs inside a real weather window. Concrete needs temperature and time to cure properly, and work pushed to the edge of the season is work that cures under conditions nobody would choose.

Practically, that means surface scopes should be assessed in late winter and early spring, when damage is visible and budgets are being set, and scheduled well before the season is fully booked. Waiting until the damage is inconvenient generally means waiting another year.

What to Ask Before Approving a Surface Scope

  • What caused this failure? If the answer is only a description of the damage, the diagnosis has not happened yet.
  • Where does water go on this site? Drainage, low spots, and catch basin condition should appear in the assessment, not just square footage.
  • Why this repair method here? Sealing, partial replacement, and full rebuild are different answers to different conditions.
  • What is the expected service life? A fix that holds one season and a fix that holds a decade are both legitimate. They should not be presented as the same thing.
  • How does this interact with winter? Snow stacking, material selection, and plow routes across the repaired area affect how long it lasts.
  • What happens to access during the work? Tenant parking, deliveries, and fire lanes are the part of the project tenants actually experience.
