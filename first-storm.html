What the First Storm of the Season Tells Tom Rosensteel About the Whole Winter

Why the first small snow event is an audit of the fall plan, what he watches during it, and why every correction it surfaces is closed before the second storm.

By Tom Rosensteel  ·  Twin City Outdoor Services

A commercial snow and ice plan is finished on paper by October. Routes are drawn, triggers are agreed, stacking locations are marked, and the contract is signed. None of that tells anyone whether the plan works. The first storm of the season does.

Tom Rosensteel, General Manager of Twin City Outdoor Services (TCOS), treats the first measurable event of the winter as an audit of everything that was decided in the fall. TCOS has served commercial properties across the Twin Cities Metro since 1997, and Rosensteel's view is that the first storm is not the start of the work. It is the first honest report on how the work was set up.

The First Storm Tests the Process, Not the Crews

Most first storms in Minnesota are small. That is exactly why Rosensteel pays so much attention to them. A two inch event will not overwhelm anyone, so if a route runs late, a priority entrance gets serviced out of order, or a property manager hears about conditions from a tenant before hearing from the provider, the weather did not cause it. The plan did.

That distinction matters because it decides what gets fixed. If a first event is written off as crews getting back into rhythm, the same seam opens again in January under twelve inches and a wind advisory, when there is no slack left in the night to absorb it. Rosensteel's approach is to assume that every miss in the first storm is a process miss until proven otherwise, and to go find the process.

Winning is a habit, unfortunately so is losing. You control the process or the process controls you. That applies to every route, every project, every outcome.Tom Rosensteel

What He Is Watching During the Event

Rosensteel is not watching the radar during a first storm. He is watching whether the decisions made in the fall held up under the first real conditions, in roughly this order.

  • Was the trigger call made when it was supposed to be made, and by the person who was supposed to make it? A plan that says service begins at a set depth or a set time before tenant hours is only a plan if the call happened that way. A call made early because someone was nervous, or late because someone was waiting for a manager, is information about the chain of command, not the snow.
  • Did the route run in the order the site plan says it should? Every commercial property has a sequence: drive lanes and fire access, main entrances and accessible routes, loading areas, then everything else. When the sequence drifts, it is usually because the operator on the truck has never seen the site plan or was handed a different one.
  • Did the piles land where the fall walk put them? Stacking locations are chosen when the landscape is visible for a reason. The first storm is when a young tree, a bed line, or a wall face gets buried by habit rather than by decision, and it is the cheapest moment of the winter to correct it.
  • How long did it take for the property manager to hear from TCOS? Not how long until service was complete, but how long until the client knew what was happening, what was done, and what was next. That gap is the single clearest measurement of whether a provider is operating as a partner or as a vendor.
  • Did the documentation come out the way the contract describes it? Timestamps, materials, conditions, and photos should arrive after the first event without anyone requesting them. If they have to be asked for in November, they will have to be asked for after the slip and fall claim in March.
Anyone can show up during a snowstorm. That is expected. What builds trust are the proactive meetings in preparation for the next season where you walk the property together and plan for the months and years ahead.Tom Rosensteel

The Debrief Is Where the Season Is Won

The part of the first storm Rosensteel considers most valuable happens after the trucks are back. Within days, while the event is still specific in everyone's mind, the operations team walks through what the plan said would happen and what actually happened, property by property.

The output is a short list of changes, not a discussion. A route that ran long gets split or resequenced. A site whose priority areas were serviced out of order gets its plan reissued to the operator who runs it. A stacking location that turned out to block sight lines or drainage gets moved before the next event, and the property manager is told why. A communication that reached the client late gets a new owner and a new trigger.

Rosensteel's standard is that no correction identified after the first storm should still be open at the second. The reason is arithmetic. A problem fixed in November costs one conversation. The same problem discovered in January costs a claim, a tenant complaint, or a client who has quietly started taking calls from other providers.

The bottom line is this: data-driven decisions beat gut feelings every time. When you can see exactly where your crews are, how routes are performing, and what the weather models say for the next 48 hours, you are not guessing. You are executing.Tom Rosensteel

Telling the Client Before They Ask

One habit Rosensteel holds his team to after a first event is to send the property manager an account of what happened before the property manager thinks to request one. What time service started, what was done and in what order, what materials went down, what was observed on site, and anything that will change before the next storm.

The point is not the report itself. It is that the property manager is accountable to tenants, ownership, and a building full of people who will have already formed an opinion about the parking lot by eight in the morning. A provider who puts the facts in the manager's hands first is protecting that manager's credibility, and that is the relationship that survives a bad winter.

Property managers are accountable to their tenants, their ownership groups, and all stakeholders in the process. They need a Partner who documents everything, responds to calls immediately, and owns the outcome. That level of accountability is often missing in this industry. It shouldn't be.Tom Rosensteel

What a Property Manager Should Watch For in a Provider's First Storm

  • Did you hear from them, or did you hear from your tenants? The order those two calls arrive in tells you most of what you need to know about the season ahead.
  • Did the priority areas get done first? Walk the site the morning after. If the loading dock was clear before the main entrance, the site plan is not in the truck.
  • Where did the snow end up? If it is sitting on a bed, against a wall, or in front of a drainage structure, ask whether that was a decision or a default.
  • Did the documentation arrive on its own? A provider who has to be asked for records after a small storm will not have them ready after a large one.
  • What did they change afterward? Ask what the debrief found. A provider with nothing to adjust after a first event either ran a flawless operation or did not look. The second is far more common.
  • Who made the calls? If the answer involves waiting for someone who was not on site, the escalation path needs to be fixed before the next event, not discussed after it.
